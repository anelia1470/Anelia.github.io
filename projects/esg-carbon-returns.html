Sustainable finance

Business model, carbon intensity and firm valuation

Do investors value carbon differently in luxury and non-luxury fashion? A panel study of 33 listed European fashion firms, 2015 to 2024.

Status
Completed, autumn 2026
Context
Term paper, BFIN4015 Sustainable Finance
Data
LSEG Workspace: 33 listed European fashion firms, 192 firm-years, 2015–2024
Methods
Panel regressions, firm fixed effects, wild cluster bootstrap, Mundlak decomposition
Tools
Stata, LSEG Workspace

The question

Fashion accounts for an estimated 4 to 8% of global greenhouse gas emissions, and EU regulation and investor attention to climate risk are both increasing. Earlier research has mostly asked whether carbon emissions affect firm value across firms and industries. In this paper, I ask whether the answer depends on the business model. Luxury and fast-fashion firms differ in pricing, production volumes and how they earn their revenue, so carbon may matter to their valuations in different ways.

Does business model moderate the valuation effect of carbon intensity? Evidence from European luxury and non-luxury fashion firms, 2015–2024.

Hypotheses

Theory points in two directions for luxury firms. Under the resource-based view, a luxury brand creates pricing power, which could let luxury firms pass carbon costs on to customers. Under the natural-resource-based view, environmental conduct becomes part of the brand itself, so rising emissions could damage the very thing luxury valuations rest on.

  • H1: carbon intensity is negatively associated with Tobin's Q.
  • H2a (buffer): the negative association is weaker for luxury firms than for non-luxury firms.
  • H2b (exposure): the negative association is stronger for luxury firms than for non-luxury firms.

Data

I built the sample from an LSEG Workspace screen of 400 European textile, apparel and retail firms, narrowed to 33 apparel and fashion firms with reported emissions. Luxury firms are those in Deloitte's Global Powers of Luxury Goods ranking. The panel is unbalanced, because emissions reporting spread over time, from 7 firm-years in 2015 to 32 in 2024.

Valuation is measured with Tobin's Q, which captures intangible value such as brand equity. Carbon intensity is Scope 1 and 2 emissions per million euros of revenue, lagged one year because emissions are published months after the year ends. Controls are size, leverage, profitability (ROA) and sales growth, plus year effects.

The raw comparison already hints at the answer. Luxury firms are valued higher (Q of 3.05 against 2.35), and their carbon intensity is about 40% lower (8.2 against 14.4 tonnes per million euros). Their absolute emissions, however, are not significantly different. Much of the intensity gap reflects luxury's higher revenue per unit rather than cleaner production.

Method

I regress ln Tobin's Q on lagged ln carbon intensity, an interaction with a luxury dummy, the controls and year effects:

ln(Q_it) = α + β1·CI_i,t−1 + β2·(CI_i,t−1 × Luxury_i) + β3·Luxury_i + γ′X_it + δ_t + μ_i + ε_it

β1 is the carbon slope for non-luxury firms, and β2 answers the research question: H2a predicts β2 > 0, H2b predicts β2 < 0. I estimate the model in two ways. Pooled OLS compares firms with each other. Firm fixed effects compare each firm with itself over time, removing stable differences such as brand heritage or country. Standard errors are clustered by firm, and because 33 clusters is few, I also report wild cluster bootstrap p-values. A Mundlak decomposition then separates the within-firm and between-firm parts of the effect.

Results

No average carbon effect

Across all firms, carbon intensity is unrelated to valuation (−0.002, p = 0.97), so H1 is not supported. This matches recent work showing that, with reported, revenue-scaled emissions, the average relation is weak. An average across business models can hide an effect that exists in only one of them.

Carbon matters for luxury firms, within firms

Once the slope can differ between business models, the picture changes. For non-luxury firms it is flat. For luxury firms it is clearly negative, which supports H2b over H2a: pricing power does not shield luxury valuations. The evidence is strongest within firms (interaction −0.396, p < 0.001, bootstrap p = 0.003), with a luxury slope of −0.389. A 10% rise in a luxury firm's carbon intensity goes with a 3.6% lower Tobin's Q. Between firms, the interaction is only marginal (−0.244, p = 0.063).

The Mundlak decomposition explains the gap between the two estimators: the within-firm part is −0.393 (p = 0.002), and the between-firm part is −0.129 (p = 0.63). Markets do not rank luxury firms by their level of carbon intensity. They react when a luxury firm's intensity rises above its own norm. This fits the legitimacy argument: what is penalised is a deviation from the brand's established image, not a stable characteristic investors already know. The flat slope for fast fashion fits too, because when a business model is already known to be high-impact, a change in its carbon intensity tells investors little that is new.

How much it matters

For luxury firms, a typical within-firm change in carbon intensity moves Tobin's Q about as much as sales growth does, and more than profitability, leverage or size. For non-luxury firms, it is the least important variable.

Robustness and what drives the effect

The within-firm interaction stays negative and significant across all the intensity-based checks: other valuation measures, excluding the COVID years, same-year instead of lagged carbon intensity, and excluding firm-years with reporting-boundary jumps. It also survives luxury-specific year effects (−0.276, p = 0.007), which remove the common luxury boom and bust of 2020 to 2024. No single firm drives it: leaving out one luxury firm at a time gives estimates between −0.349 and −0.438.

Two findings refine the interpretation. First, splitting intensity into its parts shows that, within firms, luxury valuations react strongly to revenue (0.523, p < 0.01), while emissions on their own point the same way but are not significant. Part of the effect therefore runs through the revenue side of carbon intensity, so it cannot be attributed to emissions alone. Second, with Scope 1+2+3 intensity the interaction turns positive (0.125, p = 0.02). Scope 3 has the lowest coverage and least consistent reporting, so I read this as a data limitation, and my conclusions concern direct and energy-related emissions.

Conclusion

Business model does moderate the valuation effect of carbon intensity. For non-luxury fashion firms, carbon intensity is unrelated to Tobin's Q. For luxury firms, rising intensity goes with lower valuation, and the effect is about as large as that of sales growth. The evidence supports the exposure hypothesis (H2b) and rejects the buffer hypothesis (H2a), while H1 does not hold on average.

Limitations

  • The fast-fashion group is small, because much of European fast fashion is unlisted, for example Primark and Mango.
  • The panel is small, unbalanced and weighted towards 2021 to 2024, and only firm-years with reported emissions enter.
  • Part of the effect runs through revenue, and Scope 3 data remain unreliable.
  • Some firms, such as Hugo Boss and Capri, sit near the luxury boundary.
  • The estimates are associations, not causal effects: more valuable firms may find it easier to cut emissions.

Why it matters

For investors, a single carbon screen across fashion mixes a relationship that matters for valuation with one that does not. For luxury managers, carbon performance is a valuation issue, not only a reporting one. CSRD disclosures from 2025 onward will make it possible to test the supply-chain dimension directly and to extend the analysis beyond Europe.
