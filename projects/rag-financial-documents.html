Text or tables? How chunking shapes what AI finds in financial reports

How chunk size and splitting strategy affect whether a retrieval system finds the right evidence in financial documents, and why the answer depends on where that evidence sits.

Status
Completed, autumn 2026
Context
Semester paper, BBAN4040 Language technology and large language models, NTNU
Data
FinanceBench (150 questions, 84 reports, 12,013 pages) and FinQA (1,147 test questions)
Tools
Python, tiktoken, LangChain text splitters, scikit-learn (TF-IDF), pandas, SciPy, matplotlib
Team
Co-written with Emma Emblem

The problem

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets a language model answer questions from documents it was never trained on: a retriever fetches the relevant passages, and the model answers from them. For annual reports and 10-Ks this matters, because they are long, full of tables and notes, and too big to treat as a single unit. They have to be cut into chunks first.

That choice is harder than it looks. A table row like 2014 net revenue | 5 735 means nothing without the column header, and a fixed token window can separate a row from its header or a table from the text that explains it. Retrieval is also a known bottleneck: in FinanceBench, GPT-4-Turbo with a retrieval system answered 81% of questions incorrectly or not at all.

Research question

How does the effect of chunk size and splitting strategy on retrieval quality vary between questions whose evidence is in running text, in tables or in both, and between short contexts and long financial reports?

We set three hypotheses before the analysis:

  • H1: questions with table evidence are more sensitive to chunk size than questions with text evidence.
  • H2: recursive splitting helps most when the evidence combines text and tables.
  • H3: in long reports, larger chunks give no clear gain, because relevant terms get mixed with more irrelevant text.

Data

  • FinanceBench: 150 public questions on 84 company reports (12,013 pages read with pypdf), split evenly across metrics, domain and novel questions. Retrieval runs over whole reports, with the annotated evidence pages used only for evaluation.
  • FinQA: the official test set of 1,147 numerical reasoning questions, each with text, a table and annotated supporting facts. We classified each question by where its evidence sits: text only (283), table only (706) or both (158). Tables were linearised row by row with the column header repeated, for example 2014 net revenue | amount (in millions): $5735.

Method

A full 3 × 2 design on both datasets: chunk sizes of 256, 512 and 1,024 tokens (measured with tiktoken, about 10% overlap), and two splitting strategies.

  • Fixed-token splitting cuts the token stream into fixed windows and can split a sentence or table row in half.
  • Recursive splitting (LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter) splits at paragraphs, then line breaks, then spaces, so it almost never breaks a sentence or row.

To isolate the effect of chunking, no language model generates answers. Chunks are ranked by a transparent TF-IDF retriever with cosine similarity, and quality is measured with Hit@k, Recall@k and MRR. Two controls separate real ranking gains from mechanical ones:

  • A random baseline: with few candidate chunks, even a random order often puts a relevant chunk first. We computed the exact expected score of random ranking for every question.
  • An equal token budget: one 1,024-token chunk shows the retriever four times as much text as one 256-token chunk, so we also compared conditions at the same number of retrieved tokens.

Paired comparisons use exact McNemar tests and paired bootstrap intervals, with Holm–Bonferroni correction.

Results

Short contexts (FinQA): table evidence is most sensitive to chunk size

With recursive splitting, Hit@1 rises from 0.653 at 256 tokens to 0.956 at 1,024 tokens, and every pairwise difference is significant. Splitting into larger chunks helps table questions far more than text questions: the gain from 256 to 1,024 tokens is 0.381 for table evidence against 0.163 for text evidence, a difference of 0.218 (95% interval 0.154 to 0.292).

But the random baseline rises even faster, from 0.316 to 0.836, because the number of candidate chunks falls from 4.6 to 1.4 per question. At 1,024 tokens, 61% of contexts are a single chunk. Ranking does not get better with larger chunks; the task gets easier because there is less to choose from. And at an equal budget of 1,024 retrieved tokens, four 256-token chunks hit more often (0.976) than one 1,024-token chunk (0.956).

Long reports (FinanceBench): chunking is not the bottleneck

Across all six conditions, Hit@5 stays between 0.27 and 0.31, and none of the size or strategy comparisons are significant. That is far above random ranking (about 0.09), but low in absolute terms. At an equal token budget, small chunks are clearly better: 0.28 against 0.15 at 1,024 tokens.

An error analysis shows why. At 512 and 1,024 tokens, almost every miss is a ranking failure (105 of 109 at 512 tokens): a chunk containing the evidence exists, but TF-IDF ranks it too low. One question about 3M's capital expenditure in 2018 had its evidence on the cash flow statement under "Purchases of property, plant and equipment". The right chunk was ranked 198th of 378, because a word-based retriever doesn't know the two phrases mean the same thing. Questions about standard financial metrics hit least often (Hit@5 around 0.08, against about 0.52 for novel questions).

Splitting strategy: a modest effect on small chunks

Recursive splitting was equal or better on every measure, but the difference was only clear at 256 tokens. There, fixed-token splitting broke about one in twenty evidence units (94.8% intact against 99.9%). From 512 tokens, breaks were too rare to matter.

What we found

  • H1 supported, but for a different reason than expected. Table evidence is most sensitive to chunk size, yet tables are rarely broken by recursive splitting. The problem is that TF-IDF ranks small chunks of table rows poorly: they are mostly numbers and short labels with few words in common with the question.
  • H2 not supported. The benefit of recursive splitting was largest for text evidence, not mixed evidence, and only visible at 256 tokens.
  • H3 consistent with the results. In long reports no chunk size gave a measurable advantage at fixed k, and small chunks were best at an equal token budget.

Conclusion

Chunk size should not be treated as a universal hyperparameter with one optimal value. The right choice depends on document length and structure, where the evidence usually sits, the retriever, and how many tokens the language model actually gets as context. If the model receives a fixed budget of context, small chunks with recursive splitting were the better choice in this experiment. In long reports, the bigger gains are likely to come from a better retriever, such as dense embeddings or hybrid search, rather than from tuning the chunking.

Limitations

  • A single lexical retriever (TF-IDF). Dense embeddings, reranking or hybrid retrieval could change the results, especially for tables.
  • Default scikit-learn tokenisation splits numbers at commas, which likely weakens the ranking of table rows.
  • On FinanceBench, retrieval happens within the correct report, and 150 questions give the tests low power.
  • No answer generation: a high hit rate shows the evidence is available to a language model, not that the model would answer correctly.

Why it matters for companies

Any firm putting a document assistant in front of its finance team makes these choices, usually by keeping a library default. This project shows that the default can quietly cost accuracy where the numbers matter most, and that measuring retrieval against a random baseline and an equal token budget is what separates a real improvement from an apparent one.
