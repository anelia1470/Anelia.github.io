Text or tables? How chunking shapes what AI finds in financial reports

How chunk size and splitting strategy affect whether a retrieval system finds the right evidence in financial documents, and why the search method turned out to matter even more.

Status
Completed, autumn 2026
Context
Semester paper, BBAN4040 Language technology and large language models, NTNU
Data
FinanceBench (150 questions, 84 reports, 12,013 pages) and FinQA (1,147 test questions)
Retrievers
TF-IDF, with an embedding control (BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5)
Tools
Python, pypdf, tiktoken, LangChain text splitters, scikit-learn, sentence-transformers, pandas, SciPy, matplotlib

The problem

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets a language model answer questions from documents it was never trained on: a retriever first fetches the relevant passages, and the model answers from them. If the retriever misses the right passage, the answer is wrong or incomplete, even when the information is in the document. The paper's Norwegian title says exactly that: when the information is there, but is not found.

Financial reports make this hard. They mix running text, tables and accounting figures, and they are too long to search as one unit, so they are cut into chunks first. Small chunks can make relevant information easier to find, but can separate a number from the text that explains it. Large chunks keep more context, but also more irrelevant text. A table row like 2014 net revenue | 5 735 means little without its column header.

Research question

How does document chunking affect retrieval quality in financial reports?

I set three hypotheses before the analysis:

  • H1: questions with table evidence are more sensitive to chunk size than questions with text evidence.
  • H2: recursive splitting helps most when the evidence combines text and tables.
  • H3: in long reports, larger chunks give no clear gain, because relevant terms get mixed with more irrelevant text.

Data

The two datasets complement each other. FinanceBench gives whole reports, and FinQA shows whether the evidence sits in text, in tables or in both.

  • FinanceBench: the 150 public questions on 84 company reports, read page by page with pypdf (12,013 pages). Questions are split evenly between financial metrics, general finance and report-specific questions. The annotated evidence pages were used only for evaluation.
  • FinQA: the official test set of 1,147 numerical questions, each with text, a table and annotated supporting facts. By evidence type: text only (283), table only (706) or both (158). Each table row was written out with its row name, column header and value, so tables and text could be searched the same way.

Method

A full 3 × 2 design on both datasets: maximum chunk sizes of 256, 512 and 1,024 tokens (measured with tiktoken, cl100k_base, about 10% overlap), and two splitting strategies:

  • Fixed-token splitting cuts the text into fixed windows and can split a sentence or table row in half.
  • Recursive splitting (LangChain) splits at paragraphs and line breaks first, so it rarely breaks a sentence or a row.

No language model generates answers, so the results show the effect of chunking on retrieval alone. The main retriever is TF-IDF with cosine similarity, a simple, word-based method that is easy to check. To test whether the results depend on that choice, I repeated the search with an embedding model (BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5), which compares meaning rather than shared words, on exactly the same chunks.

Quality is measured with Hit@k, Recall@k and MRR. Two controls separate real ranking gains from mechanical ones:

  • A random baseline: with few candidate chunks, even a random order often puts a relevant chunk first. I calculated the exact expected score of random ranking for every question.
  • An equal token budget: one 1,024-token chunk shows the system four times as much text as one 256-token chunk, so I also compared the conditions at the same amount of retrieved text.

An error analysis classifies every FinanceBench miss as a text-extraction, chunking or ranking failure. Paired comparisons use exact McNemar tests and paired bootstrap intervals (10,000 draws), with Holm correction.

Results

Short contexts (FinQA): bigger chunks hit more often, but the task also gets easier

With recursive splitting, Hit@1 rises from 0.675 at 256 tokens to 0.824 at 512 and 0.951 at 1,024 tokens, and every step is significant. But the random baseline rises even faster, from 0.314 to 0.833, because the number of candidate chunks falls from 4.6 to 1.4 per question. At 1,024 tokens, 62% of the contexts are a single chunk. Larger chunks make the task easier, not the ranking better. At an equal budget of 1,024 retrieved tokens, four 256-token chunks hit more often (0.978) than one 1,024-token chunk (0.951).

Table questions are the most sensitive to chunk size. With TF-IDF, their Hit@1 rises by 0.344 from 256 to 1,024 tokens, against 0.148 for text questions, a difference of 19.6 percentage points (95% interval 13.2 to 26.0). Yet almost all table evidence is intact already at 256 tokens. The tables are not being broken. TF-IDF simply ranks small chunks of table rows poorly, because they share few words with a question written in natural language. With embeddings, table questions at 256 tokens rise from 0.599 to 0.728 while text questions stay the same, and the extra gain table questions get from bigger chunks falls from 0.196 to 0.080.

Long reports (FinanceBench): chunking is not the bottleneck, the search method is

Across all six chunking conditions, Hit@5 stays between 0.280 and 0.333, and none of the comparisons are significant. That is well above random ranking (about 0.09), but low in absolute terms. At an equal token budget, small chunks are clearly better: 0.320 against 0.147 at a budget of 1,024 tokens.

Switching the search method changes far more than any chunking choice. At 512 tokens, embeddings almost double Hit@5, from 0.280 to 0.553. They find the right page for 50 questions that TF-IDF misses, against 9 the other way. For questions about standard financial metrics, Hit@5 rises from 0.14 to 0.82. By comparison, the six chunking choices differed by at most 0.053.

When the information is there, but is not found

The error analysis shows where the misses come from. At 256 tokens, about half of the misses are chunking failures, where no single chunk holds the evidence. From 512 tokens almost every miss is a ranking failure: 101 of 108 at 512 tokens, and all 102 at 1,024. The evidence is in a chunk, but the retriever ranks it too low.

One example shows why. A question about 3M's capital expenditure has its answer on the cash flow statement under "Purchases of property, plant and equipment". The two phrases mean the same thing but share almost no words, so TF-IDF ranked the right page 247th. Embeddings moved it up to 31st of 429.

Splitting strategy: a modest effect on small chunks

Recursive splitting was only clearly better at 256 tokens, where Hit@1 was 0.675 against 0.635 for fixed-token splitting. There, fixed-token splitting left all evidence intact for 89.3% of questions, against 98.8% with recursive splitting. From 512 tokens, the two strategies gave almost the same results.

What I found

Conclusion

Chunk size should not be treated as a setting with one right value. The choice should fit the structure of the documents, how much text the language model gets as context, and the search method. With a fixed context budget, small chunks with recursive splitting were the better choice in both datasets. In long reports, most misses came from ranking, not chunking, and changing the search method nearly doubled the hit rate. A semantic search method should also get chunks it can read in full: the embedding model reads at most 512 tokens, so at 1,024 tokens 67% of the FinQA chunks were cut off, and there TF-IDF did better.

Limitations

  • The embedding control uses one model and recursive splitting only, and on FinanceBench only at 512 tokens. Other models, hybrid search or reranking could give different results.
  • Default TF-IDF settings drop some numbers and short labels as search terms, which matters for accounting figures.
  • On FinanceBench, retrieval happens within the correct report, relevance is judged at page level, and 150 questions give the tests limited power.
  • No answer generation: a hit shows the evidence is available to a language model, not that the model would answer correctly.

Why it matters for companies

Any firm putting a document assistant in front of its finance team makes these choices, usually by keeping a library default. This project shows two things. Tuning the chunking matters less than choosing a search method that recognises that "capex" and "purchases of property, plant and equipment" are the same thing. And measuring retrieval against a random baseline and an equal token budget is what separates a real improvement from an apparent one.
