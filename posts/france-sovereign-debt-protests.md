## From a political confrontation to a financial question

The recent student protests in France, including the incident in which Saint-Denis mayor Bally Bagayoko stood between teenage protesters and riot police on October 6, 2026, initially appear to be a debate about educational inequality, government spending and public authority. However, the developments raise a broader economic question: how can domestic dissatisfaction translate into financial instability in one of Europe's largest economies?

It would be tempting to interpret the subsequent decline in French financial markets as a direct consequence of the protests. Yet this overlooks the economic vulnerabilities that existed long before the demonstrations began. France entered the crisis with increasing public debt, weak economic growth and difficulties reaching agreement on fiscal reforms. The protests did not create these challenges. Rather, they exposed how difficult addressing them could become.

This distinction matters because **financial markets respond not only to current economic conditions, but also to changes in expectations about the future.** A protest does not automatically reduce a government's ability to repay its debt. However, it can influence investors' expectations about political stability, public spending, economic growth and the government's willingness or ability to implement reforms.

## Sovereign debt: Is the problem the amount of debt or confidence in managing it?

According to INSEE, French public debt reached approximately €3.60 trillion, or 119% of GDP, in the second quarter of 2026. The European Commission projected economic growth of just 0.8% in 2026, alongside a government deficit of approximately 5.1% of GDP.

These figures suggest a difficult financial position, but high public debt alone does not necessarily imply an imminent sovereign debt crisis. The more relevant question is whether the government can generate sufficient future revenues and economic growth to sustain its obligations.

Debt sustainability depends on the relationship between economic growth, borrowing costs and the government's primary fiscal balance. If borrowing costs remain above the economy's nominal growth rate, stabilising the debt ratio generally requires a stronger primary balance than would otherwise be necessary.

This makes France's situation particularly interesting. The government faces pressure to reduce spending and reassure investors, while protesters are demanding greater investment in public services. Both concerns have financial implications.

From a short-term perspective, increased spending without additional revenue could worsen the fiscal deficit and increase future borrowing requirements. However, from a long-term perspective, reductions in productive public investment may weaken future growth, reducing the tax base required to service existing debt.

This raises an important question: **Could attempts to improve France's short-term fiscal position ultimately make its debt less sustainable if they weaken future growth?**

The answer depends on the type and effectiveness of spending. Education can generate economic returns through higher productivity, employment and earnings, but additional expenditure is not automatically productive. Likewise, expenditure reductions do not necessarily damage growth if they target inefficient programmes.

The underlying challenge is therefore not simply choosing between spending and austerity. It is determining which fiscal decisions improve the government's financial position over an extended period.

**What the chart shows.** Every company except TotalEnergies fell more than the CAC 40, and the order broadly follows exposure to France. The banks fell hardest, Société Générale by 2.30 points more than the index and BNP Paribas by 1.37 points more. Carrefour, the most domestic consumer business, came next. TotalEnergies was almost flat, which fits a company whose earnings depend on global energy prices, and oil was above $100 at the time.

Two results do not fit the simple story. LVMH, despite earning most of its money abroad, fell more than the index and more than Vinci. That suggests its decline reflected global luxury and growth sentiment as much as France, and supports the point that a company's listing location says little about its economic exposure. Vinci, which I expected to be sensitive to financing costs, fell only slightly more than the market.

**Why these six.** The selection is built around exposure rather than size, so that each pair isolates one channel. If the moves were purely global, all six would fall roughly as much as the index. If they were France-specific, the banks and domestic names should lead and the international names should lag. Having two companies per channel also makes it less likely that a single company's news drives the conclusion.

**Why the findings may generalise.** Each company stands for a channel, not just itself. The same channels (the link between sovereign debt and banks, domestic demand, financing costs and foreign earnings) exist in any economy where political uncertainty meets high public debt. The prediction that risk concentrates in banks and domestic businesses can therefore be tested elsewhere, for example in Italy or the UK. That is the useful part to carry forward: not the size of each move, but the order.

The limits matter too. Six hand-picked companies over two trading days make a small sample, and the period overlaps with global moves in oil prices and bond yields. Company news can also interfere: Carrefour, for instance, fell sharply on 21 October, outside this window, around its third-quarter results. The comparison suggests where the risk was concentrated; it does not prove that the protests caused the declines. A stronger test would compare each company with a European peer exposed to the same global conditions, as discussed at the end of this note.

Meanwhile, French 10-year government bond yields approached 4.9% on October 19 and reached 5% on October 21, their highest level since 2002. The yield spread over equivalent German government bonds widened to approximately 157 basis points on October 21.

From a financial perspective, a widening sovereign spread is particularly revealing. Germany and France share the same currency and are exposed to many common European monetary conditions. Therefore, the additional yield demanded on French debt suggests that investors are increasingly differentiating between the risks associated with the two governments.

However, a higher yield should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that investors expect France to default. Bond yields also reflect inflation expectations, liquidity, interest-rate risk and broader financial conditions.

Here, behavioural finance adds another perspective.

Investors do not operate with complete information about future political developments. Under uncertainty, they may place greater weight on recent, highly visible events, especially when those events suggest that existing expectations could be wrong.

The availability heuristic offers one possible explanation. Images of violent demonstrations, damaged businesses and confrontations with police may make political instability appear more immediate and consequential. Investors may consequently revise their assessment of risk even before measurable changes appear in corporate earnings or government finances.

Loss aversion could amplify this reaction. When uncertainty increases, investors may prioritise avoiding potential losses over capturing future returns. The resulting selling pressure can push bond prices down and yields up.

Yet this interpretation requires caution. We cannot conclude from falling prices alone that investors behaved irrationally or that fear caused an excessive reaction. Selling may represent a reasonable adjustment to genuinely deteriorating fiscal expectations.

**The important analytical distinction is between a market repricing risk and a market overreacting to risk.** Both can produce falling asset prices, but they have different implications for investors.

## Financial institutions: How sovereign risk spreads through the banking system

The comparatively sharp declines in French banking stocks reveal another possible transmission mechanism.

Banks are connected to sovereign debt markets through several channels. They may hold government securities, use sovereign bonds as collateral, raise funding in capital markets and provide loans to households and corporations whose financial positions are influenced by economic conditions.

When government bond yields increase, the market value of existing fixed-rate bonds declines. Depending on accounting treatment, hedging and balance-sheet exposures, this can affect banks' financial positions.

Furthermore, if investors demand a higher premium for French sovereign risk, they may also reassess the risks associated with French financial institutions. Higher bank funding costs can eventually feed through to borrowing conditions for companies and households.

This creates a possible feedback mechanism:

Higher sovereign borrowing costs can contribute to tighter financial conditions, which may reduce private investment and economic growth. Weaker growth can then put pressure on government tax revenues and make reducing the fiscal deficit more difficult.

However, the relationship is not entirely negative. Higher interest rates can benefit certain banking activities, including returns on some interest-earning assets. French banks also operate internationally and have diversified sources of revenue. Consequently, the effect of rising yields depends on each institution's exposures, funding structure and business model.

The decline in Société Générale and BNP Paribas should therefore be interpreted as evidence of increased market concern, rather than proof that sovereign debt losses directly caused their share prices to fall.

## The role of institutions: Why political credibility matters

A further issue is the relationship between political institutions and financial confidence.

France's fiscal policy does not operate independently of the European Union. Under the EU's Excessive Deficit Procedure, France is expected to correct its excessive deficit by 2029. The country's government must therefore manage domestic political demands while also demonstrating that its fiscal plans are consistent with European commitments.

This creates a difficult institutional trade-off.

Aggressive spending reductions may reassure bond investors about fiscal discipline, but they can also produce domestic opposition and make reforms politically difficult to implement. Conversely, increasing public spending in response to protests could improve certain services and reduce grievances, but may create financing concerns if credible funding measures are absent.

The financial issue is not necessarily whether a government announces an ambitious fiscal target, but whether investors believe that the government has the institutional capacity and political support to achieve it.

A policy that appears financially attractive on paper may be ineffective if it cannot pass through parliament or be implemented successfully.

The European Central Bank introduces another layer of complexity. France is part of the euro area and therefore cannot independently lower its national policy rate or depreciate a national currency to address its debt pressures.

The ECB has instruments designed to address certain forms of financial-market fragmentation, including the Transmission Protection Instrument. However, eligibility and intervention are conditional, and these mechanisms are not unconditional guarantees against rising borrowing costs.

France consequently benefits from the institutional stability of the euro area, but also faces constraints on its ability to respond independently to domestic financial conditions.

This suggests that sovereign credibility depends on more than debt statistics. It also depends on effective institutions, coherent policy decisions and the government's ability to maintain confidence among both domestic stakeholders and international investors.

## International exposure: Why France's problems do not remain French

France's position within the international financial system makes its domestic challenges particularly relevant beyond its borders.

According to Banque de France, non-resident investors held approximately 58.4% of French general government long-term debt securities at the end of June 2026.

This means that changes in international investor sentiment can materially influence demand for French government debt. These investors evaluate French bonds against alternatives such as German Bunds, Italian government bonds and US Treasuries.

If French political uncertainty increases while other investment opportunities appear more attractive, investors may reallocate capital. This can increase France's borrowing costs even without any immediate change in its capacity to service debt.

The interconnectedness of European markets was evident on October 19, when Italian 10-year government bond yields also increased. Investors were concerned that uncertainty surrounding French fiscal policy could contribute to broader reassessments of sovereign risk across highly indebted euro-area economies.

However, France's borrowing costs are not exclusively determined by domestic politics.

Global developments, particularly rising energy prices and changes in monetary policy expectations, have pushed bond yields higher across several developed markets in 2026. France's problems therefore need to be considered within an international environment already characterised by higher financing costs.

This leads to a more precise interpretation: the protests may have intensified an existing sovereign risk premium, rather than independently creating the broader rise in French borrowing costs.

France's international corporate sector adds another dimension.

LVMH, for example, generates revenue across global markets, meaning its valuation depends on international consumer demand, currencies, interest rates and market sentiment, in addition to domestic developments. The company's share-price decline cannot be explained simply by French protests, even though disruptions to retail operations and confidence may have contributed.

The same distinction applies to French banks with substantial international operations. A company's listing location does not necessarily describe the geographical distribution of its economic exposure.

## Cognitive reflection: Are markets reacting to reality or expectations?

Looking across these factors, the most important lesson is the danger of drawing a direct causal conclusion from events that occur close together.

The sequence appears straightforward: protests escalate, investors sell French assets, bond yields increase, and borrowing conditions deteriorate.

Yet correlation does not establish that the protests were responsible for the entire market reaction.

A more complete financial analysis would ask what was already reflected in asset prices before the protests, which new information became available during the escalation, and how French securities performed relative to comparable European assets exposed to the same global conditions.

For example, comparing French bank returns with those of other European banks could help distinguish the effects of France-specific sovereign risk from broader banking-sector movements. Similarly, comparing French and German bond yields is more informative than analysing French yields alone, because both markets are influenced by ECB policy and euro-area conditions.

The reaction also raises questions about how markets incorporate expectations.

Financial prices are forward-looking. They reflect not only what has happened, but also probabilities investors assign to what might happen. A protest movement may therefore influence financial markets through the perceived possibility of policy changes, prolonged instability or weaker economic performance rather than through its immediate economic costs.

However, investors may also underestimate the capacity of institutions to adapt or resolve political disagreements. If markets assign excessive weight to the most pessimistic scenarios, prices may subsequently recover without a corresponding improvement in underlying economic fundamentals.

The challenge is determining whether markets are correctly assessing future risks or extrapolating too much from recent developments.

## Final reflection: The cost of stability and the value of long-term investment

Returning to the original incident involving the French mayor and student protesters, the central issue is ultimately larger than the immediate confrontation.

The students' demands concern the quality and accessibility of public education. The government's financial challenge concerns the allocation of limited resources in an economy facing high public debt and weak growth. Investors, meanwhile, are concerned about the government's ability to maintain financial stability and implement credible fiscal policies.

These interests are connected, but they do not necessarily lead to the same preferred decisions.

From a financial perspective, spending reductions may improve certain fiscal indicators in the short term. However, if those reductions undermine human capital, economic productivity or institutional confidence, the long-term consequences may offset part of the initial savings.

Conversely, increasing education spending without considering funding, efficiency and debt sustainability could create additional financial risks.

The central question is therefore not whether France should prioritise financial stability or public investment, but **how the government can preserve fiscal credibility while supporting the economic and institutional foundations that make its debt sustainable in the first place.**

The market developments of October 2026 illustrate why this question matters. Sovereign debt, banking conditions, political institutions, investor psychology and international capital flows interact, sometimes reinforcing one another.

For financial analysis, the broader lesson is that risk cannot be understood from balance sheets and market prices alone. It also requires examining the institutions behind financial commitments, the incentives influencing political decisions, and the expectations driving investor behaviour.

**Ultimately, a country's borrowing costs reflect not only what it owes today, but also investors' confidence in its ability to make credible decisions tomorrow.**
