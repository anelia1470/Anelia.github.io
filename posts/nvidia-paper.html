Abstract

NVIDIA's market value of about $5.17 trillion (16 October 2026) is only partly justified by today's profits. The reverse DCF in this paper shows that, at an 11.7% WACC and a long-run operating margin of 55%, investors are pricing in revenue growth of roughly 21% a year from FY2027 to FY2032, taking revenue from about $400 billion to over $1 trillion. NVIDIA's economics are exceptional: return on invested capital was well above 100% in FY2026 under every definition tested, and economic profit was about $105 billion. Yet a base case with 14% growth and a 55% margin values the shares at about $155, around 28% below the market price; only a bull case, in which growth and margins both stay high, supports the current price. The conclusion is that NVIDIA creates enormous value, but the share price already discounts a long period of near-bull-case performance, and is highly sensitive to the cost of capital.

Valuation date and data. Market data are from the close on 16 October 2026 (share price $214.13, 24.15 billion shares). Financial data come from NVIDIA's Form 10-K filings and quarterly press releases filed with the SEC, through Q2 FY2027 (quarter ended 26 July 2026). NVIDIA's fiscal year ends in late January, so FY2026 covers February 2025 to January 2026. All amounts are in US dollars.

Keywords: NVIDIA, AI infrastructure, reverse DCF, ROIC, economic profit, WACC, valuation

1Introduction

The development of artificial intelligence has led to a large increase in technology investment. As more companies adopt AI, they are investing heavily in high-performance computing infrastructure. NVIDIA has played a major role in this growth, evolving from a company best known for graphics processing units (GPUs) into a key provider of AI infrastructure (NVIDIA, n.d.).

The company's financial performance reflects this. In fiscal year (FY) 2026, NVIDIA generated $215.9 billion in revenue, a 65% increase on the previous year, while Data Center revenue reached $193.7 billion. The company also earned unusually high profits: a gross margin of 71.1% and operating income of $130.4 billion (NVIDIA, 2026a, p.4). Growth has since accelerated further. Revenue in the first half of FY2027 was $177.8 billion, and the company guided to $108 billion for the third quarter alone (NVIDIA, 2026b).

This raises an important question: how long can NVIDIA maintain these profit levels? Its market position, technology and continued demand for AI infrastructure support further growth. The company also faces stronger competition, rapid technological change, supply-chain constraints and the risk of a slowdown in AI investment. The tension became visible in mid-October 2026, when a global sell-off in chip stocks was driven by doubts about whether debt-financed AI spending can earn adequate returns at higher interest rates (Oguh, 2026).

This paper examines whether NVIDIA can maintain its high profits as the AI market matures. It analyses the company's growth, margins and use of capital to determine whether it creates value for shareholders. A key focus is whether NVIDIA earns a higher return on invested capital (ROIC) than the return investors require, the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). The analysis combines financial statement analysis with an estimate of WACC, a reverse DCF, an ROIC and economic profit analysis, and a scenario DCF with sensitivity tables. The central research question is:

To what extent is NVIDIA's valuation today justified by sustainable profits and continued AI growth?

The aim is to separate value created by NVIDIA's demonstrated economic performance from value that depends on optimistic expectations of future growth. This distinction matters for investors, because high returns and rapid growth create substantial value, but they also attract competition and become harder to sustain as an industry matures (Koller et al., 2020, Chapters 8–9; Porter, 1980, Chapters 1 and 11).

2Business and competitive economics

2.1 Company overview and business model

NVIDIA, founded in 1993, first became known for GPUs used mainly for gaming and computer graphics. Today it provides much of the technology needed to build and run AI systems: GPUs, CPUs, networking products, complete data-center systems and software (NVIDIA, n.d.).

This shift has changed the composition of the business. Data Center accounted for about 88% of revenue in FY2025 ($115.2bn of $130.5bn) and about 90% in FY2026 ($193.7bn of $215.9bn) (NVIDIA, 2026a, p.79). In Q2 FY2027, Data Center revenue was $89bn of total revenue of $96.2bn, or 93% (NVIDIA, 2026b). The company's growth is therefore now tied almost entirely to the expansion of AI computing.

NVIDIA designs its chips but outsources manufacturing to foundries such as TSMC (NVIDIA, 2026a, p.8). This makes the business asset-light compared with integrated chipmakers as property and equipment was only $10.4bn at the end of FY2026, against operating income of $130.4bn (NVIDIA, 2026a, p.53). Section 7 shows that this is the main reason its return on invested capital is so high.

2.2 Competitive economics and economic profit

The reason for NVIDIA's success is that AI requires enormous computing power. NVIDIA's GPUs perform many calculations in parallel, which suits both training and running AI models. Its CUDA software platform, networking technology and rack-scale systems are designed to work together as one ecosystem. Software written for CUDA over more than fifteen years raises switching costs for customers, which makes NVIDIA's products harder to replace and gives it pricing power (Porter, 1980, chapter 1).

Estimates of NVIDIA's share of the AI accelerator market vary with method and scope, but most fall between about 70% and 80% in 2026 (James, L., 2026; Silicon Analysts, 2026). That share is under pressure from two directions. AMD competes directly in accelerated computing with its GPUs (AMD, n.d.). More importantly, NVIDIA's largest customers, Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta, have the money and engineering talent to design their own chips, such as Google's TPU and Amazon's Trainium (NVIDIA, 2026a, p.9). Custom chips can lower these customers' computing costs and their reliance on NVIDIA. Because a few hyperscalers account for a large share of NVIDIA's revenue, a shift of their spending toward in-house silicon would hit NVIDIA's growth directly.

From an investment perspective, a durable competitive advantage matters because it allows a company to earn returns above its cost of capital for an extended period (Koller et al., 2020). NVIDIA's future economic profits therefore depend not only on growth in AI demand, but on its ability to stay ahead of alternative solutions.

2.3 The AI infrastructure investment cycle

NVIDIA's revenue is, in effect, a share of its customers' capital expenditure. The four largest US hyperscalers spent about $410bn on capex in calendar 2025 and, by July 2026, had guided to roughly $730bn for 2026, an increase of about 78% (Analysis Atlas, 2026).

This spending creates the demand behind NVIDIA's results, but it also shows the main risk. Infrastructure build-outs tend to come in waves where a new technology attracts investment, capacity overshoots demand, and spending then falls back until usage catches up (Perez, 2002). The semiconductor industry is cyclical in the same way. For instance the standard deviation of operating income across US semiconductor companies over the past ten years was about 42% (Damodaran, 2026b). Bank of America estimates that the largest cloud companies will spend about 90% of their operating cash flow on capex in 2026, up from 65% in 2025 (MarketWise, 2026; Hassan, 2026), and an increasing share of the build-out is financed with debt (Oguh, 2026). If customers reach a point where existing capacity is sufficient, or if the return on additional AI investment falls, capex growth could slow sharply.

Investors must therefore distinguish between temporary growth caused by an investment cycle and structural growth caused by a permanent expansion of the market. If AI investment stays strong for many years, NVIDIA may sustain unusually high profitability. If growth normalizes, its valuation could become difficult to justify even if NVIDIA remains a highly profitable business.

2.4 Competitive economics and economic profit

The chip industry is hard to compete in. Companies must spend heavily on research, products go out of date quickly, and the largest players have big cost advantages. Even so, NVIDIA's technology lead and its software ecosystem may allow it to keep earning returns far above its cost of capital. The key financial question is not whether NVIDIA is profitable, but whether it can keep generating economic profit over time:

Economic profit = NOPAT − (Invested capital × WACC)

NOPAT is net operating profit after tax, invested capital is the capital required to run the business, and WACC is the weighted average cost of capital. If ROIC stays well above WACC, growth creates substantial shareholder value. Economic theory, however, predicts that extraordinary returns attract competitors and give customers reasons to develop substitutes, so excess returns tend to fade over time (Fama & French, 2000; Porter, 1980).

This is the central tension in NVIDIA's economics: the profitability that proves the strength of its position is the same profitability that attracts the forces that threaten it. The following sections test this quantitatively.

3Financial statement analysis

NVIDIA's revenue grew from $6.9bn in FY2017 to $215.9bn in FY2026, a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of about 47% over nine years, but almost all of that growth came after FY2023. A ten-year view is used because NVIDIA's current financial position is the result of a transformation in both its revenue base and its business model, from a gaming and graphics company into the dominant supplier of AI infrastructure. The aim is to separate structural improvements in NVIDIA's economics from growth that may belong to the current AI investment cycle.

3.1 Ten-year financial development

NVIDIA's financial development over the past decade demonstrates a significant acceleration in both scale and profitability. For most of its history, NVIDIA grew at a steady but uneven pace. That changed when generative AI took off: revenue rose from $27.0bn in FY2023 to $215.9bn in FY2026, an eightfold increase in three years.

The most important feature of the table is the acceleration after FY2023. Revenue rose from $27.0bn in FY2023 to $215.9bn in FY2026, an eightfold increase in three years. Net income rose even faster, from $4.4bn to $120.1bn. A simple extrapolation of these growth rates would produce unrealistic long-term assumptions. The data instead suggest a structural shift in NVIDIA's economics, combined with growth rates during FY2024–FY2026 that cannot remain constant.

3.2 Revenue growth and business transformation

NVIDIA's revenue history falls into three periods. From FY2017 to FY2022 it grew as a diversified semiconductor company with exposure to gaming, professional visualization, automotive and data centers. Growth was strong but uneven, and revenue fell in FY2020 after a crypto-related inventory correction. FY2023 was a transition year. Revenue was flat as gaming demand fell, while demand for accelerated computing began to rise with generative AI and large language models. From FY2024 onward NVIDIA became the central supplier of AI infrastructure, with revenue rising.

Growth has continued into FY2027. Revenue was $81.6bn in Q1 and $96.2bn in Q2, up 106% year on year, and guidance for Q3 is $108bn (NVIDIA, 2026b). On these figures, FY2027 revenue is likely to be around $400bn. Data Center revenue is now over 90% of the total. This concentration shows the strength of NVIDIA's position, but it also raises its exposure to changes in AI capital expenditure. Future revenue growth should therefore not be modelled as a continuation of the historical CAGR.

3.3 Gross profit and gross margin

NVIDIA's gross margin rose from about 57% in FY2023 to 75% in FY2025, showing how much of the value created by AI demand the company was able to capture. Before the AI boom, the margin sat around 59–65%, already high for a semiconductor company.

The decline to 71.1% in FY2026 deserves attention. NVIDIA attributed it to the transition from Hopper-based systems to full-scale Blackwell data-center systems and to a $4.5bn charge for H20 excess inventory and purchase obligations (NVIDIA, 2026a). The margin recovered to 74.9% in Q1 FY2027 and 75.0% in Q2, with Q3 guided at 74.0% as memory and wafer costs rise (NVIDIA, 2026b; CNBC, 2026a). Although a 71–75% gross margin is exceptionally high, the FY2026 dip shows that margins depend on product mix, input costs and the stage of the technology cycle. Assumptions about the normalized margin therefore have a large effect on intrinsic value.

3.4 Operating profitability and operating leverage

Operating income grew from $4.2bn in FY2023 to $130.4bn in FY2026, and the operating margin rose from about 16% to 60%. R&D spending increased from $7.3bn to $18.5bn over the same period, but fell from 27% to 9% of revenue because sales grew much faster. In the twelve months to July 2026, the operating margin reached about 65% (StockAnalysis, 2026b).

This operating leverage is one of the most important features of NVIDIA's business model. When revenue grows faster than the cost base, a large share of each additional dollar of revenue becomes operating profit. The same mechanism creates downside risk. If revenue growth slows sharply while R&D keeps rising to defend the technology lead, the operating margin could fall quickly. The scenario analysis therefore does not assume that a 60–65% operating margin continues indefinitely.

3.5 Net income and profitability

Net income rose from $1.7bn in FY2017 to $120.1bn in FY2026, showing that NVIDIA's growth translates into bottom-line profit. Net income is, however, an imperfect measure of value creation. It includes financing effects, taxes and non-operating items. In FY2026, NVIDIA's other income included $8.9bn of gains on equity investments, and in the first half of FY2027 these gains reached $23.7bn (NVIDIA, 2026a–b). Such gains are not part of the core business and are volatile. For this reason, the paper focuses on operating income, NOPAT, invested capital, ROIC and free cash flow.

3.6 Cash flow analysis

Operating cash flow rose from $1.7bn in FY2017 to $102.7bn in FY2026, broadly in line with operating profit. Capital expenditure was only $6.0bn in FY2026, so free cash flow was $96.6bn, or 45% of revenue (NVIDIA, 2026a). This indicates that earnings are backed by cash rather than accounting adjustments.

Fast growth does tie up capital in working capital. In FY2026, increases in receivables and inventories absorbed $26.7bn of cash, and in the first half of FY2027 they absorbed a further $34.8bn (NVIDIA, 2026a–b). Future free cash flow should therefore not be estimated as a fixed share of revenue, the valuation models in sections 6 and 8 link reinvestment explicitly to revenue growth.

3.7 Capital efficiency

A company can earn large profits and still create little value if it needs huge amounts of capital to do so. NVIDIA's business model is attractive from this perspective, because manufacturing is outsourced and the company does not need to own fabrication plants. Its main operating capital is working capital (receivables and inventory), not factories. If NVIDIA can keep increasing NOPAT without proportional increases in invested capital, ROIC can remain far above WACC. Section 7 quantifies this.

3.8 Overall financial assessment

The ten-year analysis shows a fundamental change in NVIDIA's economic profile: higher scale, higher margins and very strong cash generation. It also shows the main uncertainty: current performance is far above the historical average, so the recent AI-driven period cannot simply be extrapolated. The question is therefore not whether NVIDIA is highly profitable, which it clearly is, but how much of that profitability can be sustained as the AI infrastructure market matures.

4Is NVIDIA's margin profile sustainable?

NVIDIA does not need today's 60–65% operating margin to remain highly valuable, but every point of margin it loses must be replaced by additional growth. NVIDIA reported a GAAP gross margin of 71.1% in FY2026, down from 75.0% in FY2025, while its operating margin stayed around 60% (NVIDIA, 2026a). The decline was partly caused by the Hopper-to-Blackwell transition and the $4.5bn H20 charge. The recovery to 74.9% in Q1 FY2027 (against 60.5% a year earlier) and 75.0% in Q2 suggests that the FY2026 dip was largely temporary rather than a sign of structural deterioration (NVIDIA, 2026b). The analysis therefore considers a range of long-term margin outcomes rather than a single forecast.

4.1 Why margins matter for NVIDIA's valuation

NVIDIA's value depends on future free cash flow, and margins determine how much of each dollar of revenue becomes cash:

FCF = Revenue × EBIT margin × (1 − t) − Reinvestment

A lower margin directly reduces the cash generated per dollar of revenue. It does not follow that NVIDIA must keep its current margins. A lower margin on a much larger revenue base can still produce higher absolute profits. FY2027 illustrates this. If revenue reaches about $400bn at a 62% operating margin, operating income would be about $250bn, almost double FY2026, despite a slightly lower margin than the 65% of recent quarters. This trade-off between growth and margin normalization is the core of the scenario analysis.

4.2 Scenario framework

Three scenarios test how sensitive NVIDIA's value is to different combinations of growth and profitability. They are not predictions. All three start from estimated FY2027 revenue of about $402bn (first-half actuals of $177.8bn, Q3 guidance of $108bn, and Q4 assumed 8% higher than Q3). Growth is applied for five years (FY2028–FY2032), then fades to 3% by FY2037. Margins move gradually from today's ~65% to their long-term level over five years.

4.3 Bear case: Margin normalization and AI capex slowdown

The bear case assumes that the current AI investment cycle eventually normalizes significantly. Customers' AI investments cannot grow at 70–80% a year indefinitely because the return on additional capacity may fall as the installed base of computing capacity grows. At the same time, high margins invite AMD and the hyperscalers' in-house chips, and NVIDIA itself lists competition, customers' proprietary solutions and rapid technological change among its risk factors (NVIDIA, 2026b).

Under this scenario, revenue grows only about 2% a year after FY2027 and pricing pressure pushes the operating margin down to 47%. This would still be an extraordinarily profitable business by any industry standard. It is important to note that the bear case does not require NVIDIA to fail, it only requires profitability and growth to move toward more normal levels.

4.4 Base case: Strong growth with moderate normalization

The base case assumes that AI is a structural change in computing, but that NVIDIA's growth slows as its revenue base becomes very large. Revenue of about $402bn in FY2027 growing 14% a year reaches about $775bn by FY2032. The operating margin settles at 55%, a meaningful decline from the recent peak but still exceptional. Current momentum is strong as Q2 FY2027 revenue grew 106% year on year and management has indicated around 70% growth for FY2028 (NVIDIA, 2026b; CNBC, 2026a). Therefore, the base case is conservative in the short term and assumes a sharper slowdown later. Maintaining 80–100% growth from a base of $400bn would require enormous increases in absolute spending by customers.

4.5 Bull case: Sustained AI infrastructure leadership

The bull case assumes that AI infrastructure spending stays stronger for longer and that NVIDIA keeps its technological and ecosystem lead. AI demand broadens from model training to inference, agentic AI, robotics and autonomous systems, and NVIDIA's position as a supplier of complete platforms rather than single chips protects its pricing power. The product roadmap supports this. Vera Rubin entered full production in Q2 FY2027, and NVIDIA claims it can cut the cost per inference token by up to 10x compared with Blackwell (NVIDIA, 2026a–b). If cheaper computing stimulates more AI use rather than lower spending, NVIDIA benefits from a volume effect. Revenue grows 24% a year to about $1.18 trillion in FY2032, and the operating margin stays at 62%.

The crucial assumption is not that NVIDIA sells more GPUs, but that it keeps taking a large share of the economic value created by AI computing.

Growth and margins cannot be analysed separately. The scenarios produce very different operating income five years out:

Even the bear case leaves NVIDIA with operating income well above FY2026's $130bn, because the starting point for FY2027 is already so high. The base case shows that strong growth more than compensates for moderate margin normalisation. The bull case shows the earnings potential if both growth and margins remain high.

NVIDIA does not need to keep today's margins to remain highly valuable, but the lower the long-term margin, the more growth is required to support the same valuation.

4.7 Implications for the DCF valuation

Rather than using a single margin assumption, the DCF calculates value under each scenario and across a grid of growth and margin combinations (section 8). The appropriate question is therefore not “will NVIDIA's margin remain at 71%?” but “what combination of sustainable growth and normalized profitability is required to justify NVIDIA's current market value?”. The reverse DCF in section 6 answers exactly that.

4.8 Conclusion

The evidence does not support a simple conclusion that NVIDIA's margins are either permanently sustainable or bound to collapse. FY2026 showed margin pressure during a product transition, while the recovery to 75% in FY2027 suggests most of it was temporary. What matters is the interaction between margins and growth. If margins normalize toward 50–55%, NVIDIA can still create substantial value, but only with stronger and more persistent growth. If growth and margins normalize at the same time, the current valuation becomes much harder to justify.

5Cost of capital (WACC)

NVIDIA's WACC is estimated at 11.7%, almost all of it the cost of equity, because the company's debt is tiny relative to its market value. The WACC is the hurdle for both the ROIC analysis and the DCF models, so it is estimated before them.

WACC = (E / V) · re + (D / V) · rd · (1 − t)  re = rf + β · ERP

Three choices deserve comment. First, a bottom-up industry beta (1.50) is used instead of NVIDIA's own five-year regression beta of 2.22. Regression betas for single stocks have large standard errors, and NVIDIA's reflects an exceptional period in which its price moved with AI sentiment, an industry average is a more stable estimate of business risk (Damodaran, 2012). The regression beta is kept as a sensitivity. Second, the implied rather than historical ERP is used, because it reflects what investors demand today (Damodaran, 2026a). Third, the risk-free rate of about 5.1% is high by the standards of the past decade, after inflation of 3.7% in September 2026 pushed long-term yields up (CNBC, 2026b). This makes the WACC higher than many analysts would have used a year ago, and section 8 shows how much the result depends on it.

The resulting WACC reflects equity-market risk, semiconductor cyclicality and technological risk through the beta. It does not add a separate premium for customer concentration or geopolitical risk. Those risks are handled in the scenarios instead, to avoid counting them twice.

6Reverse DCF: what does the market expect from NVIDIA?

At the 16 October 2026 price, the market implies that NVIDIA's revenue will grow by about 21% a year from FY2027 to FY2032, reaching just over $1 trillion, while keeping a 55% operating margin. A conventional DCF asks what a company is worth given the analyst's forecasts. A reverse DCF works backwards, it takes the market value as given and solves for the performance needed to justify it (Mauboussin & Rappaport, 2021). This is useful for NVIDIA because a forward DCF can produce a misleadingly precise target price, while a reverse DCF makes the market's assumptions explicit so they can be judged against history, competition and industry economics.

6.1 Enterprise value as the starting point

NVIDIA's equity stakes in other companies (about $94bn) are deducted at book value because they are financial investments, not part of the operating business whose cash flows are modelled. Their market value may differ from book value, but at under 2% of the market capitalization this does not change the conclusions.

6.2 The reverse DCF framework

EV = Σt=1…10 FCFt / (1 + WACC)t + TV10 / (1 + WACC)10  TV10 = NOPAT11 · (1 − g / RONIC) / (WACC − g)

The model forecasts FY2028–FY2037 from an FY2027 revenue estimate of about $402bn, using mid-year discounting from the valuation date. Revenue grows at a constant rate for five years and then fades linearly to 3% by FY2037. The operating margin moves from about 65% today to its long-term level over five years. Tax is 17%. Reinvestment is tied to growth through a sales-to-capital ratio of 3.0: each $3 of additional revenue requires $1 of new invested capital (Damodaran, 2012). This is more conservative than NVIDIA's FY2026 ratio of about 4.4 (section 7). The terminal value uses the value-driver formula, in which growth only adds value to the extent that the return on new invested capital (RONIC) exceeds WACC (Koller et al., 2020). RONIC is set at 20%, and perpetual growth at 3%, roughly nominal GDP growth.

The variables are linked. Higher growth requires more reinvestment, so very high growth cannot be combined with very high free-cash-flow margins unless the business needs little capital. That is why the reverse DCF is tied to the ROIC analysis.

6.3 Implied revenue growth for different margins

The table shows the growth–margin trade-off from section 4 in numbers. If NVIDIA keeps a 65% operating margin, the market needs about 18% annual growth; if the margin falls to 45%, it needs about 25%. In every case, the market expects NVIDIA's revenue to more than double within five years of FY2027, to between $0.9 trillion and $1.2 trillion. For comparison, the four largest hyperscalers' entire 2026 capex budget is about $730bn (Table 1).

6.4 Implied expectations and the cost of capital

The market's required growth is very sensitive to the discount rate. At 10%, a growth rate of about 16% a year justifies the price; at the WACC implied by NVIDIA's regression beta, almost 29% a year is needed. Put differently, part of what looks like optimism about growth could instead be investors applying a lower discount rate than this paper's CAPM estimate.

The terminal value accounts for about half of the enterprise value (51% in the central case). A different test shows how demanding the price is: if revenue grew only 10% a year for five years, the price would require perpetual growth of about 8.9% after FY2037, far above any sustainable rate for a company that would by then be one of the largest in the world. That combination is not plausible, which confirms that the price depends on strong growth within the next decade rather than in the distant future.

6.5 Summary of implied expectations

The market-implied case is not extreme on any single dimension: margin falls, growth slows sharply from today, and terminal assumptions are conventional. It is, however, demanding in combination: revenue must more than double again after FY2027 while margins stay far above industry norms and capital needs stay low. Section 8 compares this with the bear, base and bull scenarios.

7ROIC and economic profit

NVIDIA earned a return on invested capital of between 150% and 327% in FY2026, depending on the definition, against a WACC of 11.7%; its economic profit was about $105bn. Rapid growth only creates value if the return on the capital needed for that growth exceeds the cost of that capital, so this section asks whether NVIDIA's growth creates value after the cost of capital, and whether its valuation rests mainly on growth, on extraordinary ROIC, or on both.

7.1 Return on invested capital

ROIC = NOPAT / Invested capital  NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − t)

Unlike return on equity, ROIC measures operating performance independently of financing (Koller et al., 2020). The benchmark is WACC: ROIC above WACC creates value, ROIC equal to WACC creates none, and ROIC below WACC destroys value.

7.2 NOPAT and invested capital

NOPAT for FY2026 is operating income of $130.4bn taxed at the effective rate of 15.1% ($21.4bn of tax on $141.5bn of pre-tax income), giving $110.7bn (NVIDIA, 2026a). Applying the tax rate to operating income rather than using net income keeps the $9.0bn of investment gains in other income out of the measure. For the twelve months to July 2026, operating income was $197.6bn and NOPAT about $165.9bn (StockAnalysis, 2026b).

As a cross-check, invested capital can also be measured from the financing side as equity plus debt minus cash and marketable securities: $44.6bn at the end of FY2025 and $103.2bn at the end of FY2026. This figure is higher because it still contains NVIDIA's non-operating equity investments and deferred tax assets.

7.3 NVIDIA's ROIC and economic profit

Under every definition, ROIC is more than ten times WACC. The capital charge is trivial relative to NOPAT, so almost all of NVIDIA's operating profit is economic profit. The explanation is the asset-light model: at the end of FY2026, property and equipment was only $10.4bn, against total assets of $206.8bn, of which $62.6bn was cash and marketable securities (NVIDIA, 2026a). NVIDIA's main operating investment is working capital, not factories.

The trend is still worth noting. Invested capital excluding goodwill rose from $24bn to $76bn in eighteen months, faster than NOPAT, as receivables and inventories grew with sales, and long-term supply prepayments rose (included in other assets). ROIC is therefore falling from extreme levels, even though it remains exceptional. The incremental sales-to-capital ratio was about 4.4 in FY2026: each dollar of new invested capital supported $4.40 of new revenue. The DCF models assume a more conservative 3.0.

7.4 Why high ROIC matters for the valuation

A high ROIC is most valuable when it can be combined with opportunities to reinvest at similar returns. Two companies growing 20% a year with WACC of 8% create very different value if one earns 10% on new capital and the other 50%: the second creates far more value from each dollar reinvested. If NVIDIA can reinvest at returns far above WACC, growth itself creates large value. If future growth needs ever larger investments while ROIC falls toward WACC, additional growth becomes less valuable. In the central reverse-DCF case, the implied return on new capital during the forecast period is about 137% (a NOPAT margin of 45.7% times a sales-to-capital ratio of 3.0), so the market is assuming that the asset-light model survives the next decade of growth.

7.5 The sustainability problem

Exceptionally high returns attract competition. NVIDIA's profitability gives semiconductor rivals reasons to develop alternative accelerators, cloud providers reasons to build their own chips, customers reasons to negotiate harder, and innovators reasons to replace existing architectures. The empirical evidence shows that company profitability tends to revert toward the mean, and that higher profitability reverts faster (Fama & French, 2000). NVIDIA's CUDA ecosystem, software, networking, scale and fast product cycles can delay this process through switching costs and barriers to entry, but not stop it indefinitely.

The investment case therefore depends on how long NVIDIA's high ROIC lasts. Very high ROIC for several years creates enormous value; sustaining it for decades would require an exceptionally durable advantage. The DCF's terminal RONIC assumption (WACC, 20% or 30% by scenario) is how this question enters the valuation.

7.6 Conclusion

NVIDIA currently creates economic profit on a scale few companies have ever achieved: about $105bn in FY2026 and over $150bn in the last twelve months. The question for investors is not whether NVIDIA creates economic profit today, but whether it can keep doing so on the capital required for future growth, and how much of that future economic profit is already in the price:

EV = Invested capital + PV(future economic profit)

With operating invested capital of about $100bn and an enterprise value of about $5,054bn, roughly 98% of NVIDIA's value is the present value of economic profit the market expects it to earn in the future.

The market price lies between the base and bull cases, a little under halfway from the base-case value to the bull-case value. The market-implied growth rate of 20.8% (at a 55% margin) is much closer to the bull case's 24% than to the base case's 14%. Even the bear case is worth about $1.7 trillion, a large valuation for a business whose revenue would barely grow after FY2027, which shows how much value NVIDIA's current earnings power supports on its own.

8.3 Sensitivity analysis

Three findings stand out. First, growth matters more than margin: moving from 14% to 22% growth adds about $70 per share at a 55% margin, while moving from a 55% to a 65% margin adds only about $25. Second, within a reasonable range of WACC (10.5–13%) and terminal growth (2–4%), the base case stays between $130 and $192, below the market price; the discount rate shifts the value by about $50 per share across this range. Third, the terminal return on new capital matters less than expected: raising terminal RONIC from 11.7% (no value from terminal growth) to 50% raises the base value only from $146 to $162, because most of the value is created during the ten-year forecast.

The price is therefore justified only if NVIDIA grows revenue by roughly 18–22% a year for five years after FY2027 while keeping an operating margin of 55–65%, or if investors apply a cost of capital well below 11.7%.

9Discussion and implications

The analysis separates NVIDIA's value into two parts: value that its current economics already support, and value that depends on a long period of near-bull-case performance. Roughly $3.6 trillion of the $5.05 trillion enterprise value is supported by the base case; the remaining $1.4 trillion, about 28%, requires growth and margins closer to the bull case. This has implications for several groups.

9.1 Implications for investors

  • The debate is about duration, not quality. NVIDIA's ROIC of 150–327% and economic profit of over $100bn leave no doubt about the quality of the business. The investment decision instead turns on how long growth of about 20% a year and margins above 55% can last. An investor who buys at $214 is effectively betting that the AI build-out continues well into the 2030s and that NVIDIA keeps most of its share.
  • The forward P/E is misleading on its own. NVIDIA trades at about 18 times forward earnings (StockAnalysis, 2026b), which looks cheap for a company growing at this rate and has been described as near multi-year lows (Bloomberg, 2026b). But those earnings reflect a peak-cycle year with 65% operating margins. The reverse DCF shows that the price still embeds substantial growth beyond FY2027; a low multiple on peak earnings is not the same as a low expectation.
  • The risk is asymmetric to the AI capex cycle. Because customers' capex is NVIDIA's revenue, a slowdown in hyperscaler spending would hit NVIDIA's growth, margins and working capital at the same time. The bear case shows a value of about $75 per share, 65% below the price. Investors should monitor hyperscaler capex guidance, the share of capex funded by debt, and NVIDIA's inventory and receivables relative to sales as early warning signs.
  • Interest rates are a direct valuation driver. With half the value in the terminal period, a one-percentage-point change in WACC moves the base value by about $20 per share. The October 2026 sell-off, triggered by higher inflation and yields, illustrates that NVIDIA's share price is exposed to rates both through the discount rate and through its customers' financing costs (Oguh, 2026).
  • Diversification matters. NVIDIA makes up a large share of major equity indices, so many investors are exposed to the AI cycle through index funds without having chosen to be. The analysis suggests that exposure should be sized as a growth bet, not as a defensive holding.

9.2 Implications for NVIDIA's management

  • Protect the moat that justifies the margin. The valuation depends more on sustained growth than on margin (Table 16). This argues for continuing to invest heavily in R&D, software (CUDA and inference tools) and full-system platforms such as Vera Rubin, even at some cost to the operating margin, because losing share to custom chips would cost more than a few points of margin.
  • Capital allocation should follow ROIC. NVIDIA returned $41.1bn to shareholders in FY2026 and about $26bn in Q2 FY2027 alone, with $99bn still authorized for buybacks (NVIDIA, 2026a–b). Buybacks create value only if the shares are bought below intrinsic value. If the base case is closer to the truth than the market price, large buybacks at current prices transfer value from remaining to selling shareholders. Investing in the business at returns far above WACC is a better use of cash, as long as such opportunities exist.
  • Watch the balance sheet's new risks. NVIDIA now holds about $94bn of equity stakes in other companies, many of them its own customers or AI developers, and has announced partnerships to mobilize over $500bn of third-party capital for AI infrastructure (NVIDIA, 2026b). These arrangements can support demand, but they also link NVIDIA's results to the financial health of its customers. If customers' access to financing tightens, demand and the value of these stakes could fall together. Transparent disclosure of these exposures would help investors judge the quality of NVIDIA's revenue.

9.3 Implications for customers, competitors and the industry

  • Customers have strong incentives to reduce dependence. Every dollar of NVIDIA's 60%+ operating margin is a cost to its customers. This is the economic reason why Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta invest in custom chips, and why their success would be the single largest threat to NVIDIA's ROIC.
  • Competitors' opportunity lies in inference. As inference becomes the larger part of AI computing, cost per token matters more than peak training performance. This is where custom chips and AMD compete most effectively, and where NVIDIA's pricing power will be tested first.
  • Value may shift along the supply chain. Rising memory and wafer costs, which are already pressuring NVIDIA's guided gross margin (CNBC, 2026a), show that suppliers such as TSMC and the memory makers can capture part of the value if capacity stays tight.

9.4 Implications for the research question

Returning to the research question, NVIDIA's valuation is justified by sustainable profits and continued AI growth only partly. The base case, built on strong but slowing growth and moderately lower margins, explains about 72% of the enterprise value. The remainder depends on outcomes closer to the bull case: an AI build-out that keeps expanding beyond the current cycle and an ecosystem advantage that withstands custom silicon for a decade. These outcomes are possible, and NVIDIA's recent execution has repeatedly beaten expectations. But they are not the most likely single outcome, and the price leaves little room for error.

10Conclusion

NVIDIA's valuation is justified by sustainable profits only in part: its current economics support roughly $155 per share, and the rest of the $214 price depends on a long period of near-bull-case growth and margins.

The financial analysis shows a genuine structural change. Revenue grew from $6.9bn in FY2017 to $215.9bn in FY2026 and is heading toward about $400bn in FY2027, with gross margins of 71–75%, operating margins of 60–65% and free cash flow of $97bn in FY2026. Because the business is asset-light, ROIC is far above the 11.7% cost of capital under every definition, and economic profit exceeded $100bn in FY2026. NVIDIA is not a speculative company; it is one of the most profitable businesses ever measured.

The reverse DCF shows what the market price requires: revenue growth of about 21% a year from FY2027 to FY2032, to over $1 trillion, with a 55% operating margin, or about 18% growth if the margin stays at 65%. The base case of 14% growth gives a value about 28% below the market price, the bull case about 35% above it, and a probability-weighted value of about $168. Growth matters more than margins, and the result is sensitive to the discount rate: a WACC near 10% would bring the required growth down to about 16% a year.

The central finding is therefore that NVIDIA can remain an outstanding company while its shares are priced for more than its most likely outcome. Whether the price proves right depends less on whether AI keeps growing, it almost certainly will, than on whether NVIDIA can keep capturing a large share of that growth at exceptional returns while its largest customers build alternatives.

11Reflections and limitations

The main lesson of this analysis is that a valuation of NVIDIA is better used to make expectations explicit than to produce a precise price target. This section reflects on the choices behind the results, their weaknesses, and what they mean for how the conclusions should be read.

11.1 Reflections on method

Why a reverse DCF. A forward DCF of a company growing 100% a year invites false precision: small changes in assumptions move the value by hundreds of billions of dollars. Starting from the market price and asking what it implies turned the question from “what is NVIDIA worth?” into “is what the market assumes plausible?”, which can be judged against history, competition and customers' budgets. The trade-off is that the reverse DCF still depends on the same model structure; it reveals the market's assumptions only under the paper's own choices of WACC, fade pattern and reinvestment.

The discount rate drives more of the conclusion than it seems. The WACC of 11.7% reflects a 10-year Treasury yield above 5%, which is unusually high compared with the past fifteen years. With a WACC of 10.5%, the base case would be about $180, and at 10% the implied growth falls to about 16%. A different author, writing a year earlier at lower rates, could have reached a noticeably more positive conclusion from the same company data. The choice of an industry beta over NVIDIA's regression beta also matters; the regression beta would have made the shares look far more expensive. The paper presents both, but the reader should note that the “overvalued” verdict is conditional on a cost of capital that markets may not be using.

The terminal value is half the answer. Between 35% and 54% of enterprise value comes from cash flows after FY2037. Using the value-driver formula, where growth adds value only through RONIC above WACC, makes this explicit and is more conservative than a simple Gordon growth model, but it cannot remove the dependence on what NVIDIA looks like in ten years, which no one can know.

Accounting choices. Stock-based compensation ($6.4bn in FY2026) is treated as an operating cost because it is part of GAAP operating income; excluding it, as NVIDIA's earlier non-GAAP figures did, would raise the value. Equity investments are deducted at book value, and the gains on them are kept out of NOPAT. These choices are consistent with Koller et al. (2020), but they are judgements.

11.2 Reflections on data and sources

Financial statements come from NVIDIA's own SEC filings, the most reliable source available, and FY2017 figures were checked against the original 10-K. Checking also revealed errors in an earlier draft of this paper, for example that FY2017 revenue was $6.9bn rather than $9.7bn, and that the illustrative scenarios of $300–500bn in revenue had been overtaken by events within months. This shows how quickly analysis of a company like NVIDIA goes out of date.

Other inputs are weaker. FY2027 revenue of about $402bn is an estimate built on company guidance. Market-share estimates for AI accelerators vary from about 70% to 85% depending on method and come from secondary sources. Hyperscaler capex figures are company guidance compiled by trackers and change almost every quarter. Market data are from a single day, during a volatile week. The conclusions should therefore be read as a structured judgement under uncertainty, not as a measurement.

11.3 Reflections on the economics

The analysis treats NVIDIA mainly through the lens of mean reversion: high returns attract competition and fade (Fama & French, 2000). There is a reasonable counter-argument that platform businesses with strong network effects and switching costs, like CUDA, can sustain high returns far longer than the average company. If that is true, the bull case deserves more weight than 25%. Historical analogies cut both ways: the railway and telecommunications build-outs created lasting value for society but poor returns for many investors at the peak (Perez, 2002), while some dominant platforms have kept their returns for decades. The paper cannot settle which pattern AI will follow; it can only show how much the price depends on the answer.

11.4 Suggestions for further research

  • Model NVIDIA's revenue bottom-up from customers' capex, AI token demand and NVIDIA's share, rather than top-down growth rates.
  • Use a Monte Carlo simulation over growth, margin and WACC instead of three discrete scenarios.
  • Compare NVIDIA with earlier infrastructure leaders at their peak, such as Cisco in 2000, to test how long excess returns lasted.
  • Analyse the effect of NVIDIA's equity stakes and financing partnerships on the quality and risk of its revenue.
