Every time you ask an AI a question, a machine somewhere has to answer it.

That machine sits in a large, windowless building, often on the edge of a small town. It runs day and night, it can use as much electricity as a small city<sup class="fn" id="cite-1"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>, and it needs large amounts of water to stay cool. Most users never see it. The people who live next to it do.

On 3 November, some of those people got a vote.

## What happened

In 41 towns and counties across ten US states, local ballot measures asked whether data centers should be allowed, restricted or banned. According to Ballotpedia, 40 of the 41 results went in the direction of limiting or blocking development.<sup class="fn" id="cite-2"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup>

The examples are strikingly similar across very different places. Alhambra, California, voted to ban data centers within the city. Peru, Maine, approved a temporary moratorium. Several Ohio towns banned large facilities, and voters in Wilmington, Ohio, rejected the rezoning of farmland for development. In Joliet, Illinois, an advisory question asking whether the city should approve AI data centers was clearly rejected.<sup class="fn" id="cite-2-2"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-3"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>

Each vote was local. Taken together, they point to something larger: the expansion of AI is no longer only a technological or financial question, but a social and political one.<sup class="fn" id="cite-4"><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-5"><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup>

## The physical side of a digital technology

AI is usually described as something immaterial. It lives "in the cloud", and from the user's side it appears almost effortless. This framing hides the fact that AI depends on physical infrastructure: land, power grids, water systems and buildings that have to be located somewhere.

For a long time, decisions about that infrastructure were made largely by companies, investors and planning authorities. The ballot results suggest that local communities now see themselves as stakeholders in those decisions, and that many of them are unwilling to accept the trade-offs involved.

## Concentrated costs, dispersed benefits

The pattern resembles what is often called the NIMBY problem, "not in my backyard". People may support a technology in general while opposing it in their own community. Most of us want reliable electricity, but few want a power plant next door.

What makes the data center case especially clear is the asymmetry between costs and benefits. The benefits of AI are dispersed: anyone with an internet connection can use it, wherever they live. The costs are concentrated: pressure on local power grids, water use, noise, the loss of agricultural land and changes to the character of a community. In economic terms, these are negative externalities, costs borne by people who are not part of the transaction.

The usual counterargument is employment. But data centers are capital-intensive rather than labour-intensive. Construction creates temporary work, while operation requires relatively few permanent staff. If the local gains are small and short-lived, and the long-term value flows to companies and users elsewhere, local opposition looks less like resistance to progress and more like a rational response to an uneven deal.

## A social licence to operate

In my sustainable finance studies, I have worked with legitimacy theory, the idea that firms depend not only on capital and regulatory approval, but on society's acceptance of how they operate. This is often described as a "social licence to operate". In [my paper on carbon intensity and luxury fashion](projects/esg-carbon-returns.html), the same idea helped explain why investors respond when a brand's environmental conduct moves away from what stakeholders expect.

The data center votes can be read as the same mechanism applied to an entire industry. The race in AI has mostly been framed around two constraints: capital and computing power. The large technology companies have access to both. These results suggest a third constraint that is harder to overcome with money alone: local consent.

Older industries know this constraint well. Energy companies, mining firms and airports have long had to negotiate with surrounding communities, build trust and share some of the benefits. The AI industry may now be entering the same phase of its development.

## Implications for the AI investment cycle

The largest technology companies are investing heavily in new data centers, and a significant part of their market value rests on the expectation that this expansion will continue.

Local resistance is unlikely to stop this expansion, but it can affect its pace, location and cost. If suitable sites become harder to secure, projects may be delayed, relocated or made more expensive through compensation to local communities. There is also a distributional concern: if development shifts towards areas where residents have less political influence, the costs of AI may fall disproportionately on communities least able to refuse them.

## Opposition to a deal, not to a technology

It would be tempting to interpret these results as a rejection of AI. That interpretation seems too simple. Many of the same voters are likely to use AI tools themselves. What they appear to reject is not the technology, but the terms on which it is being built: local costs in exchange for benefits that are mostly captured elsewhere.

From this perspective, the votes are less a sign of hostility to innovation than part of a normal democratic process through which societies negotiate the conditions for adopting a powerful technology. Not all decisions about AI will be made by firms and engineers. Some, as these results show, will be made by local communities deciding what kind of place they want to live in.

The technology sector often presents the future as inevitable. These votes are a reminder that it is not. Infrastructure has to be built somewhere, and that requires someone to agree to it.
