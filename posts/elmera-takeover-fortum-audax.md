On the morning of 25 June 2026, the shareholders of Elmera Group woke up to two pieces of news within minutes of each other.

The first: a Spanish energy company, Audax Renovables, wanted to buy the whole company. The second, published by Elmera's own board shortly after: someone else had already offered more.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-2"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup>

Four months later, Elmera belonged to the Finnish energy group Fortum and had left the Oslo Stock Exchange. Audax never even got to launch its offer.

Elmera is not a household name, but you may well be one of its customers. It is the company behind electricity brands used by about a quarter of Norway's households.<sup class="fn" id="cite-8"><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup> This post looks at how the contest was won, the way a deal team would look at it: what each side offered, what made one offer stronger, and whether the winner paid a sensible price.

## What happened, step by step

- **Spring 2026.** Audax sends private proposals to Elmera's board. They go nowhere. In parallel, the board quietly starts talks with another strategic buyer, under an exclusivity agreement.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-2"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-2-2"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup>
- **25 June.** Audax goes public. It announces that it intends to bid NOK 41.20 per share and says shareholders holding about 39% of Elmera support it. The same morning, Elmera's board tells the market that it is already in talks with a rival whose price is "significantly higher".<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-3"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-2-3"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup>
- **29 June.** The rival is revealed: Fortum offers NOK 47 per share in cash, and Elmera's board unanimously recommends it.<sup class="fn" id="cite-3"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>
- **2 October.** The offer closes. Almost 96% of the shares are tendered to Fortum.<sup class="fn" id="cite-4"><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup>
- **30 October to 6 November.** Fortum buys out the last shareholders, takes 100% ownership and delists Elmera.<sup class="fn" id="cite-5"><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-6"><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup>

## Two offers, side by side

When bankers compare competing bids, price is only the first line. The terms around it decide how likely the deal is to actually happen.

| | Audax | Fortum |
|---|---|---|
| Price (NOK) | 41.20 | 47.00 |
| Premium | 39% | 59% |
| Board backs it | No | Yes |
| Due diligence | Still needed | Done |
| Financing | Partly lenders | Own cash |
| Acceptance needed | 66.7% | Over 90% |
| Buyer | Newcomer | Nordic peer |

*Premium is to the share price before the bids. Sources: the offer announcements from Audax and Fortum.*<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-4"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-3-2"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>

Seen this way, the contest was not close. Fortum offered more money, with fewer things that could go wrong.

## Why Audax lost

### 1. It was outbid by a buyer who could afford to pay more

Fortum's offer was 14% higher per share. That is not a small gap in a takeover, and it is not an accident.

Fortum already sells electricity to around 2.3 million customers in the Nordics.<sup class="fn" id="cite-7"><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup> Buying Elmera means it can run two customer bases on one platform, one set of systems and fewer back-office teams. It expects to save and earn about EUR 30 million a year from combining the two businesses once fully in place.<sup class="fn" id="cite-6-2"><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup> Those savings, called *synergies*, are what allow a strategic buyer to pay a higher price and still make the deal worthwhile.

Audax, by contrast, described its plan as a long-term partnership where Elmera would keep its independence and brands.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-5"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> That may sound friendly, but it also means fewer savings. A buyer that does not plan to integrate the business has less room to pay a high price.

### 2. Certainty is worth a lot

Audax's offer came with a big "if". It would only launch after a satisfactory review of Elmera's books, and the money depended partly on outside investors and lenders.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-6"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>

Fortum had done its review during the weeks of exclusive talks, and it did not need anyone else's money.<sup class="fn" id="cite-3-3"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> For a shareholder, that difference matters. A slightly lower offer that is almost certain to happen can be worth more than a higher one that might never arrive. Here, the higher offer was also the more certain one.

### 3. "Support" is not a promise

The most interesting detail is Audax's claim that about 39% of shareholders supported its bid. On paper, that sounds like a strong start.

But these were statements of intention, not binding commitments. Several of the same funds, including Nordea, ODIN and Arctic, were later listed as supporting Fortum's offer instead.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-7"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-3-4"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> Fund managers have a duty to get the best outcome for their own investors. When a better offer arrived, they moved.

For anyone working in deals, this is the key lesson. A bidder counts the shares it is *sure* to get, not the ones that are friendly today. Fortum had binding pre-acceptances from Elmera's largest industrial owner and its board and management, about 7% of the shares, and the support of funds holding about 30% more.<sup class="fn" id="cite-3-5"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>

## Did Fortum pay too much?

This is the question an investment bank on the buyer's side has to answer before the board signs. A simple test is to compare two things:

- **The premium:** how much more Fortum paid than Elmera's market value before the bids.
- **The synergies:** what the expected savings are worth today.

Fortum paid about EUR 177 million more than the market price before the bids. Using Fortum's own synergy target, a gradual start from 2028 and an 8% discount rate, I estimate the synergies are worth about EUR 250 million today.

In other words, Fortum handed around 70% of the expected value of the deal to Elmera's shareholders up front. It keeps the rest, but only if the integration goes to plan, and before the cost of merging the businesses, which Fortum has not disclosed. This is quite typical. In a competitive situation, sellers capture most of the value, and the buyer is betting that it can deliver.

So Fortum did not obviously overpay, but it left itself little room for mistakes.

## Why 90% mattered

Fortum made its offer conditional on getting more than 90% of the shares. Audax asked for only two-thirds. The difference looks technical, but it shows what each buyer wanted.

Under Norwegian law, a buyer that passes 90% can force the remaining shareholders to sell, take full ownership and remove the company from the stock exchange.<sup class="fn" id="cite-11"><a href="#ref-11">11</a></sup> Fortum did exactly that. Full ownership is what makes deep integration possible. There are no minority shareholders to treat separately, and no listing to maintain. Audax's lower threshold matched its lighter, partnership-style plan, but it also signalled a smaller ambition for what it could do with the company.

## What I take from it

Looking at deals from the outside, it is easy to focus on the headline price. The Elmera contest shows that the best offer is a package: price, certainty, speed and a clear plan for what happens next. Fortum won on all four.

It also shows the quiet power of the target's board. By running an exclusive process with Fortum before Audax went public, Elmera's board, with ABG Sundal Collier as its financial adviser, had a stronger offer ready when it needed one.<sup class="fn" id="cite-2-4"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> Shareholders got a 59% premium. Whether Fortum's shareholders will feel equally happy depends on whether those EUR 30 million a year actually arrive from 2028 onwards.

Audax's public move may still have mattered. It forced everything into the open and made sure shareholders saw the competing offers side by side. We will never know whether Fortum's price would have been different without it.

**Synergies against the premium.** Run-rate synergies of EUR 30 million a year before tax, phased in by a third each year from 2028 and fully from 2030.<sup class="fn" id="cite-6-4"><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup> After 22% tax, and discounted at 8% to the end of 2026 with no growth, they are worth about EUR 251 million. The premium over the undisturbed price is about EUR 177 million, at the EUR/NOK rate implied by Fortum's own figures. That leaves about EUR 74 million for Fortum before integration costs. The result is sensitive to the discount rate: EUR 219 million at 9% and EUR 292 million at 7%.

**Process notes.**
- *Deal protection:* Elmera agreed not to seek other bids. Fortum had a right to match any superior offer, and Elmera would pay a EUR 3 million fee if the agreement ended because the board changed its recommendation.<sup class="fn" id="cite-3-6"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>
- *Fairness opinion:* ABG Sundal Collier confirmed the price was fair from a financial point of view.
- *Advisers:* Pareto Securities and BAHR for Fortum; ABG Sundal Collier and Haavind for Elmera; Alantra and DNB Carnegie, with Garrigues and Wikborg Rein, for Audax.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-8"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-3-7"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>
- *Market reaction:* Elmera's shares rose about 43% on 25 June, already pricing in a higher bid than Audax's.<sup class="fn" id="cite-10"><a href="#ref-10">10</a></sup>
