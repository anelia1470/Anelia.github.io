## In brief

- **A quiet world leader.** Silex is the world's largest pure-play maker of MEMS, the tiny sensors and moving parts inside chips. It has grown quickly and earns operating margins of around 35%.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-2"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup>
- **A big expansion ahead.** Silex is enlarging its Swedish factory and plans to convert a US chip factory, investing billions of kronor over the next four years. That may secure future growth, but it is likely to pull down profits and cash flow in the meantime.
- **The valuation looks stretched to me.** My discounted cash flow (DCF) model gives about SEK 93 per share in the base case, with a range of SEK 85–115. To justify today's price of about SEK 138, Silex would need to grow roughly 5.5 percentage points faster every year than in my base case, which already reaches its own 2030 target.

## What Silex does

Most people never see a MEMS chip, but they use them every day. MEMS stands for *micro-electro-mechanical systems*: tiny structures on a chip that can sense movement, pressure, light or sound. They sit inside medical devices, cars, data centre equipment and industrial machines.

Silex does not design its own products. It is a *foundry*: other technology companies bring their designs, and Silex manufactures them, from the first prototypes to full production. It was founded in 2000 as a PhD project at KTH in Stockholm and makes everything in one factory in Järfälla, just outside the city.<sup class="fn" id="cite-2-2"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup>

The company was listed on Nasdaq Stockholm in May 2026 at SEK 81 per share. ABG Sundal Collier was one of three joint global coordinators for the listing, together with SEB and Nordea.<sup class="fn" id="cite-3"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> The shares more than tripled in the first days, then fell back as several banks started coverage with sell ratings.<sup class="fn" id="cite-4"><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-5"><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup>

## Where the business stands

The latest report, for the third quarter of 2026, was strong.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-2"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>

- Sales grew about 22% to SEK 413 million.
- The operating margin was close to 36%, above the company's own target of "above 30%".<sup class="fn" id="cite-6"><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup>
- Operating profit beat the average analyst estimate by about 5%.<sup class="fn" id="cite-7"><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup>
- Silex had about SEK 961 million more cash than debt.

In other words, there appears to be little wrong with the business itself. The question in this note is a different one: how much of this success is already in the price?

## The investment case

### 1. Market leader in a niche with high barriers

Silex reports a 15% share of the MEMS foundry market in 2024 and says it has been the market leader for five years in a row.<sup class="fn" id="cite-2-3"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> Making MEMS at scale takes specialised equipment, process know-how built up over many years and trust from customers. These are not easy to copy, which may help protect Silex's margins.

### 2. Customers tend to stay

Eight of Silex's ten largest customers have been customers since 2017 or earlier.<sup class="fn" id="cite-2-4"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> Once a customer has qualified a chip in Silex's factory, moving production elsewhere can take years and carries risk. That may make revenue more predictable than in many other chip businesses.

### 3. Growth with a clear plan

Silex aims to reach SEK 2.5 billion in sales by 2030, up from SEK 1.4 billion in 2025.<sup class="fn" id="cite-6-2"><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup> To get there, it is expanding its Swedish clean room by about 35% and plans to convert a factory in Pennsylvania, bought for USD 40 million, into a MEMS factory.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-3"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-2-5"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> A US factory may also appeal to customers who want production closer to home.

## Valuation

I valued Silex with a discounted cash flow model. It estimates the cash the business can generate in the future and translates it into a value today.

My base case assumes:
- **Growth** of 12–13% a year to 2030, slowing after that. This roughly matches Silex's own 2030 target.
- **Operating margins** falling from about 35% today to 29% in 2028–2029, while the US factory is being built and running at a loss. They then recover to 32%.
- **Investments** of about SEK 500 million in Sweden and SEK 1.6 billion in the US through 2030.<sup class="fn" id="cite-2-6"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-7-2"><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup>
- **A required return (WACC)** of 9%, and long-term growth of 3%.

This gives about **SEK 93 per share**, roughly 32% below the current price.

### What the current price implies

A useful way to test a valuation is to turn the question around: what would have to happen for today's price to make sense?

In my model, Silex would need to grow about **5.5 percentage points faster every year** than in my base case. That would mean sales of roughly SEK 3 billion by 2030 instead of SEK 2.5 billion. Alternatively, investors would need to accept a required return of only about 7%, which seems low for a small company in a cyclical industry.

Neither is impossible. MEMS demand from medical technology, data centres and cars may well surprise on the upside. But the price seems to leave little room for delays or disappointments.

### What other analysts think

I am not alone in this view. When the banks that led the listing started covering the stock in June, SEB, Nordea and ABG Sundal Collier all set "sell" ratings, with target prices of SEK 138, 140 and 125.<sup class="fn" id="cite-4-2"><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> Since then, some have moved closer to the current price. Kepler Cheuvreux had a target of SEK 160 in July, and Nordea moved to "hold" in November with a target of SEK 140.<sup class="fn" id="cite-5-2"><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-7-3"><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup> My range is lower than all of these, mainly because I put a lot of weight on the cost of the US expansion.

## Risks

**Risks that could make the shares worth less:**
- **The US factory.** Converting a factory is complex and expensive. Silex expects it to reach roughly break-even only in 2029–2030.<sup class="fn" id="cite-7-4"><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup> Delays or higher costs could weigh on profits for years. The deal also needs approval from US authorities, which Silex applied for after the third quarter.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-4"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>
- **Customer concentration.** Silex does not publish how much of its sales come from its largest customers. If one large customer moved production or cut orders, the impact could be significant.
- **Currency.** Silex sells mainly in foreign currencies but reports in Swedish kronor. In the first nine months of 2026, growth was 28% in constant currency but only 20% in kronor.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1-5"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> A strong krona may continue to reduce reported growth.
- **One factory.** All current production is in one place. Any serious disruption in Järfälla could affect all customers at once.
- **Share sales by owners.** Silex's former Chinese owner, SMEI, still held about 10% after the listing.<sup class="fn" id="cite-3-2"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> Further sales by large owners could put pressure on the share price.

**Risks to my view, meaning the shares could be worth more:**
- Growth could be faster than expected, for example from data centre and medical customers.
- The US factory could fill up faster than planned, since some customers may prefer production in the US.
- Margins could stay closer to 35% than my model assumes.

## My reflections

Silex is the kind of company that is easy to like: a Swedish world leader, high margins, loyal customers and a clear growth plan. That is exactly why the valuation question matters. When a company is this good, the risk is often not the business itself, but paying too much for it.

What I found most interesting is how much the investment story depends on a single decision: the US factory. It may turn out to be the step that secures Silex's position for the next decade. It may also be the step that ties up capital and management attention for years. The share price today seems to assume the first outcome. I am not sure the second can be ruled out yet.

For me, the most important things to follow are progress on the US approval, whether the clean room expansion in Sweden is completed on time, and whether margins hold up as the investments begin.
