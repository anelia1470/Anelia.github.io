Every month, a small amount leaves my account and goes to Netflix. I barely notice it. It is the price of a pizza, and in return I get to watch whatever I want.

But one evening, scrolling past the same shows for the third time, I started to wonder: what if, instead of paying Netflix, I had *bought* Netflix? Not the service. The company.

So I ran the numbers.

## The experiment

The rules are simple. Imagine someone who signed up for Netflix's Standard plan in January 2015. Every month since, they paid the bill, just like millions of others. Now imagine their twin. The twin never subscribed. Instead, on the same day every month, the twin put exactly the same amount of money into Netflix shares.

Both spent the same money. One got almost twelve years of TV. The other got a piece of the company that made it.

The bill was not always the same, either. The Standard plan cost $8.99 a month in early 2015. Netflix then raised the price again and again: to $9.99, $10.99, $12.99, $13.99, $15.49, $17.99 and finally $19.99 in March 2026.<sup class="fn" id="cite-1"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup><sup class="fn" id="cite-2"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> That is more than double in eleven years. Each price increase meant the twin invested a little more, too.

## The result

After 143 monthly payments, the subscriber has paid Netflix **$1,956**.

The twin who bought shares instead has **$4,251**.<sup class="fn" id="cite-3"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>

So the twin more than doubled the money. At today's price of $19.99, that is enough to pay for Netflix for the next 17 years, and still own the shares that paid for it. The streaming habit would, in a sense, have paid for itself.

But look at the chart again. This is not a calm line going up. It is a roller coaster.

## The twist: timing is everything

If you had stopped and checked in **June 2025**, the twin's shares would have been worth **$8,442**, more than five times what was paid. That would have bought almost 40 years of Netflix.

Then the ride went down. In December 2025, Netflix agreed to buy Warner Bros.' studio and streaming business. Investors did not like it, and the share price fell. Netflix later walked away from the deal, but weak results and lower forecasts in 2026 kept pulling the stock lower.<sup class="fn" id="cite-4"><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> By October, the shares were down almost half from their record high.<sup class="fn" id="cite-5"><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup> Half the twin's paper wealth disappeared in just over a year.

And it was not the first drop. In April 2022, Netflix announced it had lost subscribers for the first time in a decade. The stock fell around 30% in one day.<sup class="fn" id="cite-6"><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup> In June 2022, the twin's shares were worth *less* than the subscriber had paid in fees. For a few months, watching TV looked like the smarter choice.

Timing also matters for when you start. Someone who began this experiment in January 2025, when the bill rose to $17.99, has so far paid $430 and has shares worth only $302. They would have been better off just watching.

## So, should you cancel Netflix and buy the stock?

Probably not, and this is the part I find most interesting.

**One company is a big bet.** The twin's result depends entirely on one company. Netflix happened to be one of the great growth stories of the past decade. Had I picked another streaming service, the story could look very different. Picking the winner in advance is the hard part, and it is easy to look clever with hindsight.

**The ride is hard to sit through.** On paper, the twin did well. In real life, would they have kept buying through 2022, when the value was below what they had paid? Or held on when half the gains disappeared in 2026? Most people would not. Staying calm during a crash is much harder than it sounds in a spreadsheet.

**The habit is the real lesson.** What really did the work here was not Netflix. It was investing a small, fixed amount every single month without thinking about it. Investors call this *dollar-cost averaging*: when prices are low, your money buys more shares; when they are high, it buys fewer. Over time, the twin's average cost was about $29 a share, while the stock now trades at about $64. The same habit, applied to a broad fund rather than one company, is how many ordinary people build wealth.

And of course, the subscriber did not lose $1,956. They bought almost twelve years of entertainment. That has value too, even if it does not show up on a chart.

## What I take from it

Netflix has done something clever: it turned a small monthly payment into something we hardly notice. The twin did the same, just in the other direction. Small, boring, automatic payments, repeated for years, can grow into something surprisingly large.

You do not have to choose between watching and investing. But next time Netflix raises its price, it might be worth asking what that extra $2 a month could become if it went somewhere else.
