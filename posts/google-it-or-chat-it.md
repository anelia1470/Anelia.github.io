For twenty years, the internet had a single front door. If you didn't know something, you googled it. The word became so common that dictionaries added "google" as a verb,<sup class="fn" id="cite-1"><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> and it travelled into almost every language: *å google*, *googeln*, *гугълвам*.

Today, more and more people say something else. They ask ChatGPT. They "GPT it". The verb has not entered the dictionary yet, but the habit has entered everyday life. That raises a question worth more than a trillion dollars: what happens to Google when people stop googling?

## How many people still google?

The short answer is: almost everyone, still. Google reported in 2025 that it handles more than 5 trillion searches a year, roughly 14 billion a day.<sup class="fn" id="cite-2"><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> In July 2025, OpenAI said ChatGPT receives 2.5 billion prompts a day.<sup class="fn" id="cite-3"><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>

Google is still more than five times larger. But the comparison flatters ChatGPT in one way and understates it in another. Not every prompt is a search: a large study of ChatGPT conversations by OpenAI and academic economists found that practical guidance, seeking information and writing together make up nearly 80% of conversations,<sup class="fn" id="cite-4"><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> so only part of that volume replaces a Google query. On the other hand, ChatGPT reached this scale in under three years, something no search engine has come close to.

There are also early signs that the balance is shifting. In 2025, Google's share of the global search market fell below 90% for the first time in a decade, according to StatCounter.<sup class="fn" id="cite-5"><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup> And in May 2025, Apple's head of services, Eddy Cue, told a US court that searches in Safari had fallen for the first time ever, and suggested people were turning to AI instead. Google publicly disputed this, saying its overall query volume was still growing.<sup class="fn" id="cite-6"><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup>

## The paradox: Google is still growing

If AI were simply replacing Google, it should show up in Google's income. It doesn't, at least not yet.

In the third quarter of 2026, Alphabet's revenue from Google Search and related products grew 12% to $63.4 billion.<sup class="fn" id="cite-7"><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup> Sundar Pichai said query growth was "the highest it's been in the last five years".<sup class="fn" id="cite-7-2"><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup> On the surface, this is a business under no pressure at all.

The explanation is that Google is not standing still. It has put AI inside search itself: AI Overviews summarise answers at the top of the results, and AI Mode lets users have a full conversation with Search. According to Google, queries in AI Mode are on average three times longer than traditional searches,<sup class="fn" id="cite-8"><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup> and the Gemini app now has more than one billion users.<sup class="fn" id="cite-9"><a href="#ref-9">9</a></sup> In other words, a growing share of "asking AI" is still happening on Google.

## But the business model is changing underneath

The details of the same earnings report tell a more complicated story.

|---|---|---|---|
| Google Search & other | 56.6 | 63.4 | +12% |
| YouTube ads | 10.3 | 11.2 | +9% |
| Google Cloud | 15.2 | 30.7 | +103% |
| Total Alphabet | 102.3 | 127.6 | +25% |
| *Search as a share of total* | *55.3%* | *49.7%* | |

*Source: Alphabet's Q3 2026 earnings release. Share of total is my own calculation.*

Three things stand out.

First, search is growing more slowly than the rest of the company. Its share of Alphabet's revenue fell from 55% to just under 50% in a single year. For the first time, the product that made Google what it is earns less than half of the group's income. The fastest growth now comes from Google Cloud, which more than doubled, largely by selling computing power and AI services to other companies. Alphabet is gradually shifting from earning money on people's questions to earning money on the infrastructure that answers them.

Second, AI answers earn less than traditional searches, at least for now. On the earnings call, Google's chief business officer, Philipp Schindler, said part of the slowdown in search growth came from "the strong growth in AI Mode queries" and "the lower monetization we were seeing in AI Mode".<sup class="fn" id="cite-8-2"><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup> A list of ten blue links has room for many ads. A single, well-written answer has far fewer natural places for them.

Third, Google is responding by bringing ads into the answers themselves. Schindler said the company is "now scaling high-quality ads within AI Overviews and AI Mode to more users and queries".<sup class="fn" id="cite-8-3"><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup> Whether users accept advertising inside an answer as readily as next to a list of links is one of the most important open questions in the industry.

## The innovator's dilemma, live

This situation is close to a textbook case of what Clayton Christensen called the innovator's dilemma.<sup class="fn" id="cite-10"><a href="#ref-10">10</a></sup> A dominant company has a highly profitable product. A new technology appears that serves users better but earns less, at least at first. If the incumbent ignores it, a competitor takes the market. If it embraces it, it cannibalises its own best business.

Google has chosen to embrace it. That is probably the right decision: it is better to lose some revenue per query to your own AI than to lose the query entirely to someone else's. But it comes with a cost. Generating a written answer requires running a large language model, which is computationally far more expensive than retrieving a ranked list of pages. Google is therefore moving towards a product that is more expensive to deliver and, so far, harder to monetise. The fact that its query volume keeps growing suggests users like the change. Whether shareholders will like it as much depends on whether advertising inside AI answers can match what the old search page earned.

## What investors made of it

The day after Google presented its latest results, on 29 October, its shares had their worst day since the company went public in 2004. About $500 billion of its market value disappeared in a single trading day.<sup class="fn" id="cite-11"><a href="#ref-11">11</a></sup>

Why would investors react so strongly to a company whose search business is still growing? The answer has less to do with people leaving Google, and more to do with what it now costs Google to keep them.

Giving AI answers to billions of people requires enormous data centres filled with expensive chips. In the last quarter, Google spent more than twice as much on building them as it did a year earlier. In fact, it spent more on building than its business brought in, so more money flowed out of the company than came in.<sup class="fn" id="cite-7-3"><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup> Profits were also lower than investors had expected.<sup class="fn" id="cite-12"><a href="#ref-12">12</a></sup>

At the same time, Google itself admitted that AI answers earn less than ordinary searches, because one written answer has less room for ads than a page of links.<sup class="fn" id="cite-8-4"><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup>

Put simply, investors are worried about both sides of the same problem. Google is spending much more to produce each answer, while each answer may earn it less. They are not doubting that people still use Google. They are doubting whether the new, AI-powered Google will be as good a business as the old one.

## What it means for the rest of the web

There is one more group affected by this shift, and it has less power than Google: the websites that search used to send people to.

In a study of real browsing data, Pew Research Center found that when a Google search showed an AI summary, users clicked on a traditional result in 8% of visits, against 15% when there was no summary. Only 1% of visits included a click on a link inside the summary itself, and users were more likely to end their browsing session altogether.<sup class="fn" id="cite-13"><a href="#ref-13">13</a></sup>

For news sites, forums and guides, which depend on search traffic, this is a serious threat. And it creates a long-term question for AI itself: if fewer people visit the sources, fewer people may produce the information that AI answers are built on.

## "Google it" will survive, but it will mean something else

I don't think "google it" is disappearing. Words often outlive the habits that created them: we still "dial" a number and "hang up" a phone. What is changing is the act behind the word. Searching used to mean looking for pages. Increasingly, it means asking for an answer.

The most interesting finding, to me, is that Google's biggest competitor in this shift may be Google itself. People are not only leaving Google for ChatGPT. Many are asking AI inside Google, and that is reshaping its business from within: from ads next to links towards ads inside answers, and from selling attention towards selling computing power.

The question is no longer whether people will keep googling. It is whether Google can earn as much from answering questions as it did from pointing to them.
